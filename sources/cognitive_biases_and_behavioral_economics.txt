Cognitive Biases, Heuristics, and Behavioral Economics

Cognitive psychology and behavioral economics examine the systematic deviations from normative rationality that characterize human judgment and decision-making. Pioneered by Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, Prospect Theory challenges the classical economic assumption of the rational utility-maximizing agent (Homo economicus) by demonstrating that human choices are systematically distorted by cognitive heuristics, mental shortcuts, and loss aversion.

Dual-Process Theory posits that human cognition is governed by two interacting cognitive systems: System 1 operates automatically, fast, intuitively, and with little or no voluntary effort, whereas System 2 allocates attention to effortful mental operations, complex computations, conscious reasoning, and critical self-monitoring. While System 1 heuristics are computationally efficient for rapid survival, they frequently generate predictable cognitive biases.

Prominent heuristics and biases include:
1. The Availability Heuristic: People assess the probability of an event based on the cognitive ease with which relevant instances come to mind, leading to distorted risk perceptions.
2. The Representativeness Heuristic: Probabilities are evaluated by the degree to which an object or event resembles a typical prototype, often neglecting base rate statistical probabilities.
3. Anchoring and Adjustment: Initial arbitrary numeric anchors exert an outsized influence on subsequent quantitative judgments and price negotiations.
4. Loss Aversion: Psychological studies reveal that the pain of losing is approximately twice as intense as the pleasure of gaining equivalent value.

In public policy and marketing, Choice Architecture and Nudge Theory leverage these behavioral insights to guide human behavior predictably without forbidding options or significantly altering economic incentives.
